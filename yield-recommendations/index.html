Recommendation 1 · price

Stop paying for yield with price

Aid per entering student more than doubled in ten years and yield did not rise. A smaller pool will add pressure to discount. Stop raising aid by default, keep need apart from price, and test each new award.

See the evidence ↓ Recommendation 2 · the admit pool

Admit for fit, not volume

Offers grew two and a half times while the class stayed near 500. A smaller pool ends that. Size the pool to the class, build early commitment and follow the funnel state by state.

See the evidence ↓ Recommendation 3 · the offer

Lead with what peers cannot match

Most peers offer most of the same majors. The law school, the graduate school and a few rare programs are what they lack, and a reason for a student in another state to come.

See the evidence ↓ Recommendation 4 · the requirements

Turn requirements into promises

Lewis & Clark asks for more language and more years on campus than most peers, and more of its class now comes from Oregon. Make each one come with something students get.

What standing still would take

Arithmetic, not a forecast. It assumes Lewis & Clark's applications and offers shrink in step with the West's graduating class between 2025 and 2041, and that nothing else changes.

1

Stop paying for yield with price

Stop raising aid per entering student by default, keep aid that meets need apart from aid that cuts the price, and spend new aid only where it is shown to change a student's decision.

How strong is the evidence? Strong that aid rose and yield did not, at Lewis & Clark and at its peers. It is not proof of what yield would have been without the aid, and most of the increase went to students with financial need.

The evidence

  • Aid per entering student rose from $22,614 to $50,811 between fall 2015 and fall 2025, and the price a first-year paid after aid fell 44% after inflation, from $30,065 to $16,803. Yield went from 14.1% to 12.5% and never rose above 15.4%.
  • The undergraduate discount rate rose from 58.0% in FY2024 to 63.1% in FY2026. Gross undergraduate tuition and fees barely moved ($135.8M to $135.0M), so undergraduate net tuition fell from $57.1M to $49.8M, $7.3M less in two years.
  • Peers did the same and got the same. From FY2019 to FY2024, 29 of 32 peers raised their discount rate, and only 4 of them gained yield. The median peer raised its discount 6.6 points and lost 4.0 points of yield. Among the 92 colleges U.S. News ranks above Lewis & Clark, 76 raised their discount and 18 of those gained yield.
  • Occidental went the other way over the same years: it lowered its discount rate from 44.9% to 40.0% and kept yield near 20%.
  • Price is not a contest Lewis & Clark can win against its richest peers. Its endowment per student is $99,478, against $676,014 at Reed, $522,840 at Whitman, $433,902 at Kenyon and $383,089 at Occidental.

What the shrinking pool changes

  • Aid doubled while the pool was still growing. Aid per entering student went from $22,614 to $50,811 between fall 2015 and fall 2025, the years in which the national graduating class climbed to its peak. Yield did not rise then, and from here each class comes from a smaller pool.
  • Every college is discounting more. Among the 258 private colleges in NACUBO's 2025 study, the average first-year discount rate was an estimated 57.1% for 2025-26, up from 48% ten years earlier. A smaller pool adds to that pressure at every college, and a price cut that holds one class would have to be repeated for each smaller class after it.
  • The plainest price cut is the merit award to a student without financial need. Its average more than doubled, from $15,836 to $35,287, while list tuition rose 51%. The share of the class receiving one did not grow: 33% in fall 2015 and 27% in fall 2025.

What to do

  • Stop raising aid per entering student by default. It has gone up in every year but one since fall 2015, and a shrinking pool will invite it to go up again.
  • Keep two budgets: grants that meet need, and merit awards to students without need. The second was $5.0M for the fall 2025 class and is the part most plainly spent on price, so it is the first place to stop raising by default.
  • Do the student-level study this site describes: yield by aid offer, need and merit for admitted students, from records the college already holds.
  • Run new aid as a test. Change an award for a defined group, keep a comparison group, and keep the award only if enrollment responds.

How to tell if it is working

  • Aid per entering student ($50,811 for fall 2025) and the first-year discount (75.1%) stop rising.
  • Yield by aid band, reported every fall.
  • Four-year net tuition per entering student, which fell from $100,477 for the fall 2015 class to $77,013 for the fall 2022 class, in FY2025 dollars.
  • Merit awards to students without need: how many and how large (142 students at an average of $35,287 for fall 2025).

What the data cannot show

Aid is not set at random. It may have kept yield from falling further: applications fell 26% from their fall 2016 peak. Year-by-year totals cannot separate the two; student records with a comparison group can.

Each of the last four entering classes received more aid than the one before. As they replace earlier classes the overall discount rate can keep rising even if the next class receives no more, so judge the change by the entering class.

Whether the students in a smaller pool will need more aid or less. Lewis & Clark's own class has needed more: Pell-eligible students were 16% to 22% of each entering class from fall 2015 to fall 2023, and 32.9% of the fall 2025 class. Nor can public data show what other colleges will offer as their own pools shrink.

2

Admit for fit, not volume

Size the admit pool to the class, build the share of students who commit to Lewis & Clark early, and follow the funnel state by state as the pool shrinks.

How strong is the evidence? Clear in Lewis & Clark's own counts over 24 years. The smaller admit pool is one year of evidence, and the pattern across peers is loose. The projections rest on children already born, but they count graduates, not applicants.

The evidence

  • Admission offers grew from 1,970 for fall 2001 to 4,967 for fall 2024, two and a half times as many. The entering class went from 416 to 482. It took 4.7 offers to enroll one student in 2001 and 10.3 in 2024.
  • For fall 2025 the college made 836 fewer offers (4,131) and enrolled 35 more students (517). Yield rose from 9.7% to 12.5%.
  • Binding early decision is a small part of the class. 73 students were admitted early decision for fall 2025, at most 14% of the class, and between 39 and 73 in every year since fall 2015.
  • If every one of those 73 enrolled, the other 4,058 admitted students produced 444 enrollments, a yield of 10.9%.
  • Across the 34 schools, the more selective a school is, the higher its yield tends to be (correlation −0.48). At Lewis & Clark's admit rate that pattern gives a yield of 14.4%. Lewis & Clark's was 11.7%.

What the shrinking pool changes

  • Volume is the lever a smaller pool takes away. Applications are already 26% below their fall 2016 peak (7,796 to 5,804), a fall that came while the national graduating class was still growing. If applications shrink with the West's graduating class, making the fall 2025 number of offers in 2041 would mean admitting 89% of applicants instead of 71%.
  • The class leans more on Oregon, and Oregon is about to shrink. Oregon students were 9% to 12% of U.S. first-year students in every fall from 2015 to 2021, and 22% in fall 2025. Oregon's graduating class is projected to be 8% smaller in 2030 than in 2025 and 22% smaller in 2041, and the ten Oregon and Washington peers recruit from it too.
  • What was lost is reach. About 474 first-year students a year came from other states in the fall 2015 to 2019 classes, and about 368 a year in the last two. Among the college's other top home states, California is projected to graduate 28% fewer students in 2041 than in 2025, Colorado 14% fewer and Washington 11% fewer. Texas is about level.

What to do

  • Plan the admit pool from the class size and each admitted student's likelihood of enrolling, not from volume. Fall 2025 is consistent with the last several hundred offers adding few students.
  • Set a goal for the share of the class that comes through early decision and other firm early commitments, and report it every fall. It has been at most 7% to 15%.
  • Treat signs of interest (visits, interviews, contact with faculty) as part of the admission read, and track yield by each.
  • Report applications, offers, yield and enrolled students by home state every fall, and set recruiting effort against each state's projected pool.

How to tell if it is working

  • Admission offers per enrolled student: 8.0 for fall 2025, against 7.0 at the median peer for fall 2023.
  • Early decision admits as a share of the class (at most 14.1% for fall 2025).
  • Class size, net tuition per entering student and second-year retention (83.0% for the fall 2024 class), so that yield is not raised simply by shrinking the class.
  • U.S. first-year students from Oregon (22% for fall 2025) and from other states (about 380).

What the data cannot show

Yield rises on paper whenever fewer students are admitted. The goal is the class and its net tuition, not the ratio.

Selective schools are selective because more students want them. The peer pattern shows what demand looks like, not a tactic that creates it. The public data also say nothing about which admitted students were close to enrolling.

Why fewer students come from other states: fewer applications, fewer offers or lower yield. Federal data report only the home states of students who enrolled, every other year, and the rest is in the college's records. Whether Lewis & Clark can win students in states where the pool is holding is untested, and every college with a shrinking home market will look to the same places.

3

Lead with what peers cannot match

Put the law school, the graduate school and the few programs peers lack at the front of the offer, most of all for students in other states, and count who comes for them.

How strong is the evidence? Clear on what peers offer and on where net tuition is growing. There is no public measure of whether prospective students want the rare programs.

The evidence

  • The list of majors does not set Lewis & Clark apart. The typical Group 1 peer offers 25 of its 30 majors (83%), and 60% of 2020 to 2024 graduates majored in a program that at least 30 of the 33 peers also offer. Psychology, the largest major at 16% of graduates, is offered at all 33.
  • The majors few peers offer are small. Computer Science and Mathematics (2 of 33 peers) and World Languages (6 of 33) together account for 3.5% of graduates. International Affairs, long a signature major, fell from 10.0% of graduates in 2005 to 2009 to 7.9% in 2020 to 2024, and 22 of 33 peers now offer it.
  • What an undergraduate college cannot copy is the rest of Lewis & Clark: a law school and a graduate school of education and counseling. From FY2024 to FY2026 net tuition rose 7.3% at each of them while it fell 12.8% in the undergraduate college.
  • The bridges exist. The college offers a six-year BA and JD, with admission to the law school guaranteed to students who keep a 3.5 grade point average and meet its other benchmarks; a BA and Master of Studies in Law in environmental law in as little as four years; and a five-year BA and Master of Arts in Teaching. It markets them together as the 4-5-6 Commitment. Several rare minors point the same way: Law and Policy (9 of 33 peers), Earth System Science (5) and Entrepreneurial Leadership and Innovation (12).
  • Having them has not been enough. These routes date from about 2019, and yield was 12.0% for fall 2019 and 12.5% for fall 2025. The college's published list of those initiatives gives no enrollment or yield target for them.

What the shrinking pool changes

  • The undergraduate college is the part that depends on 18-year-olds. It brought in 60% of Lewis & Clark's net tuition in FY2026. The law and graduate schools enroll older students, and demand for law school is rising: 27.4% more people applied to law schools nationally in the 2026 cycle than two years earlier.
  • Programs beyond the bachelor's degree are what colleges built the last time the pool shrank. In the decade after 1981 the number of 18-year-olds fell by a fifth or more. Between 1984 and 1994 the number of four-year colleges offering programs beyond the bachelor's degree grew by 155, and graduate enrollment grew sharply. Lewis & Clark already has both schools.
  • A distinctive offer is a reason to come from farther away. The U.S. students Lewis & Clark has lost are from other states: about 474 a year in the fall 2015 to 2019 classes and about 368 a year in the last two, while the number from Oregon nearly doubled. A student in California or Colorado has many colleges nearer home, and most offer the same majors. Forecasts by Nathan Grawe put the pool for colleges ranked outside the U.S. News top 100 about 10% lower and describe a gentler path for more selective colleges. Lewis & Clark is tied at No. 97 among national liberal arts colleges.

What to do

  • Choose two or three routes where the law or graduate school adds something an undergraduate college cannot, and make them the first thing a prospective student sees, above all in other states. Law and policy and the environment are the strongest candidates: each joins a minor few peers offer to the law school. Teaching, through the graduate school, is a third.
  • Give each route its own numbers every year: inquiries, applications, admits, yield and students enrolled, by home state.
  • Test demand before adding programs. Rare is not the same as wanted.

How to tell if it is working

  • Applications and yield among students who name one of the routes as their interest.
  • Students entering the BA and JD, BA and Master of Studies in Law, and BA and Master of Arts in Teaching routes each year.
  • Graduates in the programs few peers offer, 3.5% of graduates today for the two rarest majors.
  • First-year students from other states (about 380 for fall 2025), and how many of them name a route.

What the data cannot show

Whether admitted students know these routes exist, how many use them, or whether they would choose Lewis & Clark for them. None of that is public.

Across the 34 schools, how much of Lewis & Clark's catalog a school shares has no reliable relationship with its yield, and neither does its share of graduates in computing, engineering, business and health. A distinctive catalog does not raise yield by existing.

Graduate study is not a shelter by itself. In preliminary national counts for fall 2026, master's enrollment fell 3.7% and new international enrollment fell 15%.

4

Turn requirements into promises

Lewis & Clark asks more of its students than most peers do, and more of its class now comes from Oregon, where every regional peer asks less. Make each requirement come with something students can see they get.

How strong is the evidence? Clear on what each school requires. Across peers there is no sign that requirements move yield in either direction, so this is about how the requirements are presented, not whether to keep them.

The evidence

  • Every student must reach the 201 level in a language, three semesters for a student starting from nothing. 28 of 33 peers require less, and 7 require none.
  • Students must now live on campus for six semesters, up from four. 17 of 33 peers require less, including all ten peers in Oregon and Washington, which require four.
  • Requirements do not sink yield. Across the 34 schools neither one has a measurable relationship with yield. St. Olaf requires as much language as Lewis & Clark and all four years on campus, and has the highest yield of the 34 (26.6%).
  • The requirements are not yet presented as benefits. When read in November 2026, the catalog, the Campus Living page and the 2026-27 housing agreement still described four semesters, and the general education page did not say whether AP or IB scores meet the language requirement.
  • The other half of each promise already exists. By the college's own count about 60% of undergraduates study overseas, and 69% of undergraduates lived in college housing in fall 2025 under the four-semester rule.

What the shrinking pool changes

  • More of the class can compare the rules directly. Oregon students are 22% of U.S. first-year students, up from 9% to 12% through fall 2021, and all ten Oregon and Washington peers require four semesters on campus to Lewis & Clark's six. As Oregon's graduating class shrinks, those peers will be recruiting the same smaller group.
  • More of the class will feel the cost. A third of the fall 2025 class is eligible for a Pell grant (32.9%). The share was between 16% and 22% in every class from fall 2015 to fall 2023. A third required year in college housing is a cost a family can price, so the promise has to include the price and what aid covers.
  • More families are new to this. First-generation students averaged 15% of the fall 2015 to 2019 classes and were 22.4% of the fall 2025 class. One rule, stated the same way everywhere, matters more when fewer families have been through college admission before.

What to do

  • Present the language requirement as preparation for overseas study, and pair it with a stated commitment: a place on an overseas program for every student who wants one.
  • Say what the third year on campus includes (a guaranteed room, its price, the housing open to juniors, and what aid covers) before students apply. Every Oregon and Washington peer asks for one year less.
  • Correct the catalog, the Campus Living page and the housing agreement so admitted students read one rule, and publish every way to meet or test out of the language requirement, including for students who already speak a second language.

How to tell if it is working

  • Yield among admitted students from Oregon and Washington, where the residency rule can be set directly against every regional peer.
  • The reasons admitted students give for enrolling elsewhere, from a survey of admitted students.
  • The share of undergraduates in college housing (69% in fall 2025) and the share who study overseas.
  • Yield among Pell-eligible and first-generation admitted students, from the college's records.

What the data cannot show

How admitted students weigh either requirement. The peer comparison shows only that heavy requirements and high yield can go together.

The six-semester rule is new, so no class has yet enrolled under it. What a third required year of housing costs families, and whether the college can place every student who wants to go overseas, are not in this analysis.

Whether a third required year changes the decision for Oregon students or for families with financial need. Both are a growing share of the class.

What all four depend on

Each recommendation is a reading of public totals: classes, schools and years. None of them can say which students respond to what. The college already holds the records that can: who applied, who was admitted, what each was offered, who visited, who enrolled and who stayed. The most useful step is to analyze those records by student and to try each change on a part of the class before applying it to the whole. The shrinking pool sets the pace: the West's graduating class is projected to be 6% smaller in 2030 than in 2025 and 20% smaller in 2041, so the next five entering classes are the time to learn what works.

How the peer groups were chosen

The same three groups are used on every peer page of this site. Willamette is in groups 1 and 2 and is counted once, in group 1. "Oregon and Washington" marks the ten peers in those two states, across all three groups: Gonzaga, Pacific Lutheran, Pacific University, Reed, Seattle Pacific, Seattle University, University of Portland, University of Puget Sound, Whitman and Willamette.

What this page is

A reading of the public data collected on this site, with a small number of figures added from the same public sources. It is not a statement by Lewis & Clark College, and it is not a forecast. The recommendations are hypotheses to test, in the same spirit as the cautions on What the Discount Bought.

Every figure is a published number or is computed directly from published numbers. Where one rests on an assumption, the sentence says so.

Revised November 20, 2026 to add the projected decline in high school graduates, where the entering class comes from, and what the shrinking pool changes for each recommendation.

  • The pool: projected high school graduates from WICHE, Knocking at the College Door, 11th edition (December 2024), tables 4, 5 and 9, as on The Shrinking Pool. Changes on this page are measured from the class of 2025. The top five home states are from the college's Class of 2029 page. The fall 2026 count of 18-year-old undergraduates is from the National Student Clearinghouse.
  • Where the class comes from, and its need: the share of U.S. first-year students from out of state (item F1) and first-year students by race, ethnicity and citizenship (item B2) from the Common Data Sets; first-generation and Pell-eligible students from the CAS Trends factbook of October 2025 and, for fall 2025, the Class of 2029 page; need-based grants and merit awards (items H2 and H2A) as on What the Discount Bought. The link for each year is in the entering classes CSV.
  • Added for this page: early decision applications and admits (item C21) and the share of undergraduates in college housing (item F1) from Lewis & Clark's Common Data Sets, 2015-16 to 2025-26, read November 20, 2026. The link for each year is in the entering classes CSV.

Cautions

  • Thirty-four schools is a small set. A correlation inside the shaded band cannot be told apart from no relationship (5% level, two-sided), and one outside it shows that two things move together, not that one causes the other.
  • Yield falls when applications rise faster than enrollment and rises when the admit pool shrinks, whatever students think of the school.
  • Language and residency requirements are today's rules, set against fall 2023 yield. Six schools, Lewis & Clark among them, have changed their residency rule since 2023.
  • Peer discount rates include graduate and law tuition where a school has them. Lewis & Clark's undergraduate rate (63.1% in FY2026) is higher than its college-wide rate (55.0%). On the undergraduate-only basis that nine Group 1 audits allow, its FY2025 rate of 60.6% was higher than six of the nine (see Undergraduate Tuition Discounting).
  • The early decision and housing figures were read from the Common Data Set files by an automated reader. In most editions the numbers print apart from their labels and were matched by the form's fixed order; the applicant, admit and enrolled totals in the same files match this site's figures in every year. The fall 2024 and fall 2025 editions both print 98 early decision applications.
  • The pool figures are projections of high school graduates, not of applicants. A selective private college may see a smaller change: WICHE projects private high school graduates in the West to be about level, and Grawe's forecasts describe a gentler path for more selective colleges. The standing-still figures assume applications move with the number of graduates. The national counts for fall 2026 are preliminary.
  • Oregon and other-state counts are worked out from a percentage published to the nearest whole number, and are good to within a few students. The out-of-state share was read from the same files, by the same automated reader, as the housing share, and each edition's housing figure matched the one already on this site. The 2018-19 edition prints 80% out of state for all undergraduates, well below the years on either side; the first-year figure used here is 89%.
  • The account of how colleges adapted to the decline of the 1980s is from one study. The national race and ethnicity projections count public high school graduates only.
