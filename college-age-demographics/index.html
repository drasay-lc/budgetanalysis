The Shrinking Pool: College-Age Demographics

Built with Claude Fable 5.2, November 2026, from a literature and data source search. The highlighted states are the top five home states the college lists for its fall 2025 class. The calculator's starting shares are an example, apart from Oregon's; see the commit history.

The number of students finishing high school each year has peaked. This page shows how far the pool is projected to fall by 2041 in the West and in the five states Lewis & Clark lists as the top home states of its fall 2025 class, how the makeup of the graduating class changes, and what a given mix of home states implies for the college's own pool.

The bottom line

  • The decline is already set. Everyone who will finish high school by 2041 has been born. The projections rest on counted births, not on a forecast of them.
  • The West falls furthest. Its graduating classes are projected to shrink 20%, against about 10.5% for the country and a small gain in the South.1,4
  • California is the largest exposure and Oregon gives no shelter. California falls 29% and Oregon 19%. Washington (8%) and Colorado (12%) hold up better, and Texas grows 5%.1
  • The class changes as it shrinks. Hispanic and multiracial graduates are the only groups projected to grow nationally.3
  • The usual offsets are not offsetting. Births fell again in 2025 and new international enrollment fell 15% this fall.5,8

What the research adds

  • No rebound in births is expected. The fall since 2007 runs across nearly every group of women and is not explained by any single economic or policy change after the recession.13
  • Small colleges feel it most. Among public four-year institutions, those that award only bachelor's degrees have been about five times as sensitive to state demographic change as flagship research universities.12
  • Selectivity matters. Demand at selective colleges is projected to hold up better than at regional ones, because more future students have college-educated parents.14
  • Speed matters as much as size. A 15% fall arriving at once could close up to 80 more colleges; the same fall spread over five years adds about five closures a year.11
  • It has happened before. The downturn of the 1980s and early 1990s was at least as steep, and most colleges adapted.12

Cautions

  • These are projections, not audited figures. Births come from birth certificates counted by the CDC. Graduate counts for future years are modeled by WICHE from births, school enrollment and graduation rates.
  • Two baselines are in use. State and regional changes run from the class of 2023. The national 13% figure runs from the 2025 peak. They should not be mixed.
  • The projections predate recent immigration changes. WICHE published in December 2024.
  • Several recent figures are reported through news coverage of the original releases, and the fall 2026 enrollment figure is preliminary. The source table marks which.
