Where Net Tuition Peaks: A Plain-Language Guide

The college gives back 55% of its list-price tuition as scholarships. Would it bring in more money by giving back less? This page walks through the answer one idea at a time, using only public data from FY2015 to FY2026 (the entering classes of fall 2015 to fall 2025). No economics background needed.

The bottom line

  • Today: in FY2026 the college listed $185.5M of tuition and fees, gave back $102.1M as scholarships (a 55.0% discount) and kept $83.4M.
  • The trade-off: giving back less raises what each student pays, but some students would then choose another school. Which effect wins depends on how price-sensitive students are.
  • What 2015 to 2025 shows: the price an entering student paid after scholarships fell 44% after inflation, yet the share of admitted students who enrolled went from 14.1% to 12.5%. The best estimate of price sensitivity from these classes is 0.00: no measurable effect. The estimate is uncertain; the realistic range runs from 0 to about 1.1.
  • The answer: if the best estimate holds, giving back less loses no students. At the period's own 45.9% average discount, FY2026 net tuition would be $100.4M, $17.0M more than today. At 0.56, the middle of the realistic range, net tuition peaks near a 37% discount at about $7M more than today, with about 22% fewer students. Only near the top of the range (about 1.1) is today's 55% close to the best choice.
  • What retention adds: aid doubled, but the share of each class returning for a second year stayed between 81.5% and 86.3%, and the shares still enrolled in years three and four did not rise either. Each entrant is enrolled about 3.3 of the first four years, so an entering student is worth about $55,700 in net tuition over four years at the fall 2025 price, and the fall 2025 class about $28.8M. Because retention does not measurably respond to price, the four-year view gives the same peak as the one-year view.
  • The catch: aid rose while applications fell, which pulls the measured sensitivity toward zero. The true number is probably above 0, so the gains above are likely on the high side.

STEP 1Net tuition is students times price

Every college sets a list price, the tuition on the website: $67,614 for undergraduates in 2025-26. Most students pay less, because the college gives them scholarships out of its own budget. The share of list price given back is the discount rate. What is left is net tuition, the money the college actually keeps.

Net tuition = number of students × what each pays after scholarships

The discount pulls these two numbers in opposite directions. A bigger discount lowers what each student pays, but it attracts more students. A smaller discount raises what each student pays, but some students go elsewhere. The question is which pull is stronger.

A grocery store faces the same question when it puts milk on sale: more customers buy, but each pays less. Whether the sale makes money depends on how many extra customers it brings.

STEP 2Price sensitivity in one sentence

Price sensitivity (economists call it elasticity) is the percent of students you lose when the price after scholarships goes up 1%. A sensitivity of 0.5 means a 10% price increase loses 5% of students. A sensitivity of 1.0 means it loses 10%.

0.00

Two everyday examples: gasoline has low sensitivity, because people still need to drive when the price rises. Restaurant meals have high sensitivity, because people can cook at home. Colleges sit in between. Students care about price, but they also care about fit, programs and location, and every college offers discounts.

STEP 4Why net tuition has a peak

Plot the box's area for every discount and, at most sensitivities, you get a hill. At a very low discount, the price is high but too few students come. At a very high discount, plenty of students come but each pays little. Somewhere in between is the top of the hill: the discount that brings in the most money. At a sensitivity of exactly 0 there is no hill: every point of discount cut is pure gain, because no students leave. That is why the uncertainty in Step 5 matters so much; try 0.56 or 1.12 to see the hill form.

The rule for finding the top: net tuition is at its peak when a 1% price increase loses exactly 1% of students. Below that point, raising the price gains more from students who stay than it loses from students who leave.

STEP 5How sensitive are Lewis & Clark's students?

Everything above depends on one number, so it matters how it was measured. The college publishes enough each year to compare the price an entering class paid after scholarships with how many admitted students chose to enroll. That share is called yield.

If students are price-sensitive, years when the price after aid was lower should show higher yield. The chart shows each entering class from fall 2015 to fall 2025. A cloud that slopes down to the right means students respond to price; a flat cloud means they do not.

Each entering class: price after aid vs share of admitted students who enrolled

The price after aid fell from $30,100 (fall 2015) to $16,800 (fall 2025), in 2025 dollars. Yield moved between 9.7% and 15.4% with no upward trend.

From the dots to one number

A raw chart can mislead, because other things change over the years too. So the estimate compares classes while holding two things steady: how many students were admitted (a bigger admitted pool means a bigger class at any price) and a gradual long-run drift that affects many private colleges. In statistics this is a regression. Its result comes with a standard error, a measure of how far the estimate could be off: the true value is very likely within about two standard errors of the estimate.

Comparing yield alone with price, instead of class size, gives the same answer: no measurable effect. The page uses four values from the realistic range: 0.00 (the best estimate), 0.30, 0.56 (the middle of the range, one standard error above the estimate) and 1.12 (the top, two standard errors above).

Why the true number is probably above zero

Since 2015 the college raised aid steeply while demand was falling: applications dropped from 7,796 in fall 2016 to 5,804 in fall 2025. If aid went up because fewer students were interested, the extra aid and the weaker interest arrive together and hide each other in the data. That pulls the measured sensitivity toward zero even if aid does help. Eleven classes is also a small sample. So read 0.00 as "these years show no clear payoff from more aid," not as proof that students ignore price.

What the model leaves out

  • Costs. Fewer students also means lower costs, which would favor a lower discount. It also means less housing and dining revenue, which would favor a higher one. The model counts tuition only.
  • Who gets the aid. The model treats the discount as one number. In practice, cutting merit awards would likely lose students faster than cutting need-based aid.
  • Time. A change reaches one entering class a year, so the full effect takes about four years.
  • Shape. The model assumes a straight demand line. That is reasonable near today's discount and less reliable far from it, which is why the range stops at 30% and 65%.
  • One sensitivity for everyone. It is measured on first-year undergraduates and applied to the whole college, including Law and the Graduate School, which discount far less.
  • Few years. Eleven entering classes is a short record, which is why the realistic range is wide.

STEP 7Does more aid keep students? What retention adds

Yield tests whether aid gets students in the door. Retention tests whether it keeps them: the share of an entering class that is back the next fall. The college publishes it for every CAS class in its factbook, along with the shares still enrolled in years three and four.

If aid were buying classes that fit the college better, those classes would stay longer, and retention would have climbed as aid doubled. The chart repeats Step 5 with retention instead of yield.

What retention cannot tell you

The question that matters most is who leaves when the discount falls. If the students a lower discount would turn away are the same ones who leave after a year anyway, losing them costs much less than the model assumes, and today's aid is partly buying one-year stays. If instead the heavily aided students are the ones who persist, cutting need-based aid costs more than a uniform cut suggests. Public data cannot separate these. A table of retention by aid amount, and by merit versus need, is an internal admissions query and the first thing to ask for. Merit aid grew fastest after 2016, so whether merit recipients stay is the first row to read.

Cautions: ten classes with a 2.6-point spread is a small, noisy sample; the fall 2019 class (78.5%) had its second year in the pandemic; and a returning student's price depends on year-two aid, which is not published, so the entering price is only a rough stand-in.

STEP 8The four-year view

Steps 3 and 4 count one year of net tuition. A student who stays pays again. With Lewis & Clark's rates since fall 2015, 83.6% of a class returns for year two, 75.5% is there in year three and 72.5% in year four, so each entrant is enrolled about 3.3 of the first four years. At the fall 2025 price after aid ($16,803), that makes one entrant worth about $55,700 in net tuition over four years, and the fall 2025 class of 517 about $28.8M.

Four-year net tuition = entrants × price after aid × years enrolled (1 + year 2 share + year 3 share + year 4 share)

Why this changes the stakes but not, by itself, the answer. If every class faces the same discount, the college's net tuition in any year is just entrants times price times years enrolled. So the hill in Step 4 is already the four-year hill, as long as retention does not depend on price. If it does, a lower discount loses students twice, at the door and in later years, and the peak moves toward today. The second slider lets you try that. The measured response is about 0.5 points per 10% price rise, with a realistic range of 0 to about 1.8.
