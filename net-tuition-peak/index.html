Where Net Tuition Peaks: A Plain-Language Guide

The college gives back 55% of its list-price tuition as scholarships. Would it bring in more money by giving back less? This page walks through the answer one idea at a time, using only public data. No economics background needed.

The bottom line

  • Today: in FY2026 the college listed $185.5M of tuition and fees, gave back $102.1M as scholarships (a 55.0% discount) and kept $83.4M.
  • The trade-off: giving back less raises what each student pays, but some students would then choose another school. Which effect wins depends on how price-sensitive students are.
  • What the data say: Lewis & Clark's own history from 2001 to 2021 suggests a moderate sensitivity (about 0.64). Classes since 2015 show no measurable response to price at all, though that number is uncertain.
  • The answer: at 0.64, net tuition peaks at about a 42% discount, roughly $4M a year more than today, with about 18% fewer students. At any sensitivity below 1.0, today's 55% is past the peak. Only at 1.0 or higher is today's discount the best choice.

STEP 1Net tuition is students times price

Every college sets a list price, the tuition on the website: $67,614 for undergraduates in 2025-26. Most students pay less, because the college gives them scholarships out of its own budget. The share of list price given back is the discount rate. What is left is net tuition, the money the college actually keeps.

Net tuition = number of students × what each pays after scholarships

The discount pulls these two numbers in opposite directions. A bigger discount lowers what each student pays, but it attracts more students. A smaller discount raises what each student pays, but some students go elsewhere. The question is which pull is stronger.

A grocery store faces the same question when it puts milk on sale: more customers buy, but each pays less. Whether the sale makes money depends on how many extra customers it brings.

STEP 2Price sensitivity in one sentence

Price sensitivity (economists call it elasticity) is the percent of students you lose when the price after scholarships goes up 1%. A sensitivity of 0.5 means a 10% price increase loses 5% of students. A sensitivity of 1.0 means it loses 10%.

0.64

Two everyday examples: gasoline has low sensitivity, because people still need to drive when the price rises. Restaurant meals have high sensitivity, because people can cook at home. Colleges sit in between. Students care about price, but they also care about fit, programs and location, and every college offers discounts.

STEP 4Why net tuition has a peak

Plot the box's area for every discount and you get a hill. At a very low discount, the price is high but too few students come. At a very high discount, plenty of students come but each pays little. Somewhere in between is the top of the hill: the discount that brings in the most money.

The rule for finding the top: net tuition is at its peak when a 1% price increase loses exactly 1% of students. Below that point, raising the price gains more from students who stay than it loses from students who leave.

STEP 5How sensitive are Lewis & Clark's students?

Everything above depends on one number, so it matters how it was measured. The college publishes enough each year to compare the price an entering class paid after scholarships with how many admitted students chose to enroll. That share is called yield.

If students are price-sensitive, years when the price after aid was lower should show higher yield. The chart shows each entering class. A cloud that slopes down to the right means students respond to price; a flat cloud means they do not.

Each entering class: price after aid vs share of admitted students who enrolled

From the dots to one number

A raw chart can mislead, because other things change over the years too. So the estimate compares classes while holding two things steady: how many students were admitted (a bigger admitted pool means a bigger class at any price) and a gradual long-run drift that affects many private colleges. In statistics this is a regression. Its result comes with a standard error, a measure of how far the estimate could be off: the true value is very likely within about two standard errors of the estimate.

Why the recent number is probably too low

Since 2015 the college raised aid steeply while demand was falling: applications dropped from 7,796 in fall 2016 to 5,804 in fall 2025. If aid went up because fewer students were interested, the extra aid and the weaker interest arrive together and hide each other in the data. That pulls the measured sensitivity toward zero even if aid does help. The older, longer period has more variety in prices and gives a firmer estimate, so this page uses 0.64 as the default and lets you try others.

What the model leaves out

  • Costs. Fewer students also means lower costs, which would favor a lower discount. It also means less housing and dining revenue, which would favor a higher one. The model counts tuition only.
  • Who gets the aid. The model treats the discount as one number. In practice, cutting merit awards would likely lose students faster than cutting need-based aid.
  • Time. A change reaches one entering class a year, so the full effect takes about four years.
  • Shape. The model assumes a straight demand line. That is reasonable near today's discount and less reliable far from it, which is why the range stops at 30% and 65%.
  • One sensitivity for everyone. It is measured on first-year undergraduates and applied to the whole college, including Law and the Graduate School, which discount far less.
